What If Tech Corporations Bore the Cost of Consent?

Modeling Data Center Growth Under Alternative Community Approval Regimes

By Dhruv Sheth, Lauren Pryor, Tyler Gatewood, Amrita Pasupathy

March 2026

The question isn't whether communities will resist. It's whether we build a system where resistance is the only recourse.

As data centers rapidly emerge to meet the demands of modern technology, many communities struggle to provide the power, water, and land resources necessary to support such aggressive expansion. This project explores how changes in consent-based policy affect data center creation in U.S. communities.

Data centers are heavily on the rise in the United States, especially with the increasing popularity of Artificial Intelligence in recent years. In 2025, U.S. data center capacity was already approximately 10.2 gigawatts. By 2035, it is projected to reach 71.8 gigawatts - a seven-fold increase (ABI Research, January 2026). Cushman & Wakefield estimated 25.3 GW was already under construction in the Americas at the end of 2025 alone.

This rapid acceleration has come with benefits and drawbacks. Some communities view data centers positively, as they bring increases in tax revenue and employment. On the other hand, these facilities consume enormous amounts of water for cooling, place significant strain on local power grids, and transform the character of the communities they enter.

Residents of Northern Virginia - home to the largest data center concentration in the world - have documented sharp rises in electricity costs, aquifer depletion, and the conversion of agricultural land. Virginia's Joint Legislative Audit and Review Commission (JLARC) found in 2024 that while data centers generate substantial tax revenue, the benefits and costs are distributed unevenly across communities, and that saturation effects are already visible in hyperconcentrated counties.

Even if communities were to become more informed of the long-term downsides, the current approval process treats data center siting as a routine permitting matter. Community consent, if it exists at all, is passive: residents must organize, fund legal challenges, and sustain pressure campaigns to block projects already in motion. This silences the voices of the community by placing a higher barrier to participation in decision-making.

Research Question: What if tech corporations bore the cost of obtaining consent for data center construction, rather than communities bearing the cost of resistance?

To investigate this, we construct a simulation framework that models U.S. data center growth from 2026 to 2035 under a range of consent regimes - from laissez-faire to supermajority-voting requirements to firm-borne consent mechanisms - and measures how growth trajectories, spatial distributions, and community outcomes change across scenarios.

This work functions as a formalizer in the sense described by Abebe et al. (2020): we use technical modeling to make the implicit structure of the existing consent process explicit and measurable, rendering its distributional consequences visible. It also operates as a rebuttal, challenging the unstated assumption that the current process is neutral. As Narayanan (2022) argues, quantitative methods that treat observed outcomes as fair baselines risk laundering existing power structures into algorithmic objectivity.

The Current Landscape

Before modeling the future, we need a picture of the present. The FracTracker National Data Centers Tracker documents approximately 337 hyperscale and megascale facilities across 232 U.S. counties, representing roughly 10.2 GW of installed capacity.

The concentration is striking. Northern Virginia alone hosts roughly 600 facilities. Texas, Georgia, and the Carolinas are the next tier. Meanwhile, many states with favorable conditions - low water stress, cheap electricity, available land - have almost no data center presence. This geographic inequality is both the starting point for our simulation and one of its key outcome measures.

Five Alternative Consent Regimes

We operationalize “consent” through approval thresholds - the share of the local population (at the county level) that must affirmatively support a project before it can proceed. Currently, no such threshold exists in most jurisdictions. Our simulation introduces one and varies it across five scenarios:

S1

Laissez-Faire

The status quo: minimal oversight. All candidates are built.

S2

Simple Majority

Mandatory 50%+ community approval for site authorization.

S3

Supermajority

Elevated 75% hurdle reflecting regional ecological impact.

S4

Firm-Borne (50%)

Corporation funds the approval process with simple majority threshold.

S5

Firm-Borne (75%)

Corporation funds full supermajority consensus building.

In firm-borne scenarios, corporations can invest in local benefit-sharing - tax relief, employment guarantees, infrastructure investment - to raise the local approval rate above whichever threshold applies, at a cost they bear. The simulation records what mix of tax relief and employment guarantees they choose, and at what cost. This is the core intervention.

A Two-Column Methodology

The core modeling challenge is that we do not have direct survey data on what share of people in each U.S. county support a new data center. Instead, we must infer a county-level approval probability from observable features. We approach this through two complementary models - one data-driven, one theory-driven - that serve as mutual checks on each other.

Data-Driven Model (XGBoost)

  • Train on 108 counties with known outcomes
  • Learn nonlinear feature-approval relationships
  • Rank feature importance from data
  • Extrapolate to all 3,153 counties
  • Generalizes from observed outcomes with no manual parameter assumptions

Limitation: Prone to inherent training data biases and overfitting due to small sample size.

Theory-Driven Model (Generative)

  • Inspired by Lombardi Networks & domain knowledge
  • Uses survey data to assign explicit penalties/bonuses per feature
  • Construct approval probability from first principles
  • Transparent causal assumptions
  • Explicit and debatable assumptions with interpretable causal reasoning

Limitation: Assumes penalty structure from theory; may miss real-world nonlinearities.

The calibrated probability for each county is then parameterized as a Beta(α, β) distribution for stochastic sampling in the Monte Carlo simulation.

Generative Approval Model

As a complement to the data-driven approach, we developed a generative model that constructs county-level approval probabilities from first principles. Rather than learning feature weights from training data, we draw on readings about power dynamics and community resistance to choose three key loci of intervention for modeling community approval:

  • Drought (penalty) - represents the stress households face when water resources are already limited, which data centers then amplify. A resource limitation signal.
  • Proximity to resistance (penalty) - captures protection against information asymmetry. Nearby documented community resistance shifts power imbalances by drawing attention to impacts that might otherwise go unnoticed.
  • Unemployment (bonus) - functions as a desperation index. Communities with high unemployment are more receptive to job creation narratives, even though data centers may not actually create proportional employment.

These penalties and bonuses are assigned explicitly to each county, and the resulting approval probability is constructed as a weighted sum. The transparency of this approach is its strength: every assumption is visible and debatable, creating a framework where stakeholders can argue about whether the drought penalty is calibrated correctly rather than arguing about whether consent “matters” in the abstract.

Nonlinear Intervention Functions

In firm-borne scenarios, two nonlinear intervention channels capture how corporate investment shifts local approval. Both share a structural feature: the marginal impact decreases as the number of existing data centers in the county increases (saturation).

  • Tax-benefit curve: Exponential decay A·exp(−λ·n). Strong when n is small (early development); decays toward zero as the county saturates. Calibrated using the CEA Florida figure of $405.8M in annual tax revenue per GW.
  • Employment-benefit curve: Bell-shaped L·(n/n₀)·exp(1−n/n₀). Peaks at moderate facility counts, falls off as communities question whether data centers are net employment generators. Inflection near n=8-12, matching where Northern Virginia commentary shifted from pro-development to skeptical.

Monte Carlo Simulation Engine

The simulation runs in monthly time steps from January 2026 through December 2035 (120 steps total), repeated across 10,000 independent Monte Carlo draws per scenario.

01

Generate Candidates

~1.5 GW/month drawn from the baseline queue, distributed across states by EIA electricity share.

02

Assign to Counties

State share × county weight. Counties selected proportional to existing facility density.

03

Sample Approval

Draw from Beta(α, β) centered on the county's calibrated probability, adjusted for intervention shifts and saturation penalties.

04

Apply Regime Rule

Laissez-faire: draw > 0.5. Voting: draw > threshold. Firm-borne: LP-optimized investment to clear threshold at minimum cost.

05

Update Saturation

If built: n → n+1 for that county. Intervention deltas decay with n. Community utility and firm cost recorded.

Current Snapshot: What Vanishes?

If we had always required consent, how many of today's 337 facilities would exist? We compute a counterfactual 2025 baseline for each scenario. For every county with existing data centers, we ask: given its structural features, what is the probability that a simulated community vote would have approved construction?

Under laissez-faire, all 337 facilities remain - 10.2 GW. Under a simple majority, roughly 134 survive (4.1 GW). Under a 75% supermajority, only 23 (0.7 GW) would have cleared the bar. When firms invest in consent (S4), 284 survive (8.6 GW). Even under the strictest firm-borne regime (S5), 94 facilities (2.9 GW) remain.

Each scenario's simulation then starts from its own counterfactual baseline and projects 10 years of growth forward.

The Cost of Consent

When firms invest in community consent, they pay real costs - tax incentives calibrated against $405.8M/GW (CEA Florida), plus employment guarantees modeled on 45,367 construction jobs and 5,322 permanent jobs per GW. But those investments generate community surplus that vastly exceeds the cost.

Community surplus = cumulative tax revenue + employment value ($M). Firm cost = cumulative consent investment ($M). 10,000 MC draws.

Key Findings

1

Consent doesn't have to kill growth

Firm-borne consent at 50% increases capacity by 62% (358 facilities, 107.5 GW vs. 300 facilities, 90.1 GW under laissez-faire). When firms invest in communities, they unlock counties that would otherwise block development.

2

We can visualize and model harm formally

Unemployment functions as a community's desperation for jobs (even though more jobs aren't necessarily created). Community resistance shifts power imbalances by drawing attention. Drought represents the stress households face when resources are limited, which data centers amplify. By modeling these dynamics, we make tradeoffs visible and debatable.

3

The current system is not neutral

Laissez-faire assigns all resistance costs to communities. Our simulation makes this visible so that the tradeoffs can be recognized and debated honestly. The consent cost that firms bear in S4 and S5 is not a burden added to a neutral system - it is the cost of something that communities are currently providing for free.

Summary of Results

Counties Modeled

3,153 counties with extrapolated approval probabilities. 232 with existing data center presence (FracTracker). 108 used for XGBoost training.

Monte Carlo Scale

10,000 independent draws × 120 months × 5 scenarios. Explicit RNG with deterministic per-draw seeds for full reproducibility.

Key Metrics

Total facilities built, cumulative GW, Gini coefficient (geographic concentration), community surplus ($M), firm consent cost ($M).

Sensitivity Analysis

±30% variants on intervention coefficients test robustness of findings to uncertainty in behavioral parameters.

Conceptual Framing

Structural Efficiency

  • The current “laissez-faire” regime treats data center creation as the default
  • Communities bear all costs of resistance: legal fees, organizing, pressure campaigns
  • Approval is passive - no formal mechanism for community voice

Relational Consent

  • A consent-based approach requires companies to externalize projected environmental impacts before planning approvals
  • Firms internalize the cost of obtaining approval
  • Communities gain a formal mechanism for democratic input

Our project most directly instantiates the formalizer role described by Abebe et al. (2020). We take a social and political process - community consent over infrastructure - that currently exists only informally, and build a technical model that makes its parameters explicit and its distributional consequences measurable. We are also aware of the critique Narayanan (2022) raises: our model is deliberately counter-hegemonic in its framing. We do not treat laissez-faire as a neutral baseline; we treat it as a policy choice, comparable in kind to the alternatives we model.
